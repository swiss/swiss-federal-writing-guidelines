## Chapter 1 — Spelling

### §1-01 — British English by default
**Rule:** Use British English by default. Use US English only if the target readership is known to be American.
> ⚠️ Exception: FDF financial statements and treasury reports use US English.
✅ organisation, colour, centre
❌ organization, color, center

### §1-02 — -ise / -yse
**Rule:** Use `-ise` (not `-ize`) and `-yse` (not `-yze`).
✅ prioritise, organise, paralyse, analyse
❌ prioritize, organize, paralyze, analyze

### §1-03 — programme vs program
**Rule:** `programme` for TV/documents; `program` only for computer software.
✅ TV programme / computer program
❌ TV program / computer programme

### §1-05 — Double consonants
**Rule:** A final `-l` is doubled after a short vowel when adding `-ing`, `-ed`, or `-er`.
✅ travelling, travelled, traveller / levelling, levelled, leveller
❌ traveling, traveled, traveler
> Exceptions (no doubling): focus, combat, target, parallel, develop
> ✅ focusing, combating, targeting, unparalleled, developing

### §1-06 — -ct- not -x-
**Rule:** Use `-ct-` not `-x-` in derivatives.
✅ connection, reflection
❌ connexion, reflexion
> Exceptions: complexion, flexion

### §1-07 — metre vs meter
**Rule:** `metre` = unit of length; `meter` = measuring instrument.
✅ five metres / a gas meter
❌ five meters / a gas metre

### §1-08 — gram, kilogram, tonne
**Rule:** Write `gram`, `kilogram`, `tonne` (metric). `ton` = non-metric. Never write `metric tonne`.
✅ 5 kg / 10 tonnes
❌ 10 metric tonnes / 10 tons (if metric intended)

### §1-09 — judgement vs judgment
**Rule:** `judgement` in general contexts; `judgment` in legal contexts.
✅ a poor judgement / the court's judgment

## Chapter 3 — Capital letters

### §3-01 — Title vs descriptor
**Rule:** Use upper case for titles, lower case for descriptors (typically preceded by 'the').
✅ Federal Councillor Viola Amherd / the federal councillor
✅ In Kyiv today, the president of the Swiss Confederation, Ignazio Cassis, held talks with President Volodymyr Zelenskyy.
❌ the Federal Councillor announced / Federal Councillor Amherd

### §3-02 — 'Federal Councillor' with full name
**Rule:** The title 'Federal Councillor' precedes first name + surname, not surname alone.
✅ Federal Councillor Viola Amherd → thereafter: Ms Amherd
❌ Federal Councillor Amherd

### §3-03 — Capitalisation within titles
**Rule:** In titles and names, capitalise nouns, adjectives, adverbs and verbs. Leave lower case: articles, conjunctions, prepositions, possessive adjectives and pronouns. In a subtitle, only the first main word is capitalised.
✅ The Federal Department of Economic Affairs, Education and Research
✅ The Seventh Pan-European Environmental Assessment: Summary for policymakers
❌ The Federal Department Of Economic Affairs, Education And Research

### §3-04 — Long descriptive names
**Rule:** The longer and more descriptive a name, the fewer capitals. Names that read more like a description use lower case.
✅ The federal programme for equal opportunities between men and women
✅ Committee for the adaptation to technical progress of the directive on the introduction of recording equipment in road transport

### §3-05 — Organisation names and titles of works
**Rule:** Organisation and programme names take initial capitals — no inverted commas (unlike French/German). Titles of works take single inverted commas.
✅ The next EU Framework Programme for Research and Innovation is called Horizon Europe.
✅ Every year we update the publication 'The Swiss Confederation – a brief guide'.
❌ The next EU Framework Programme is called 'Horizon Europe'.

### §3-06 — Parliament / Government
**Rule:** `Parliament` (the institution, no article) = upper case. `the parliament` (the system, with article) = lower case. `Government` = always lower case with article.
✅ In today's session, Parliament debated the new health bill.
✅ The Swiss parliament has two chambers.
✅ The government today ratified the agreement.
❌ The Government today ratified / The Parliament has two chambers.

### §3-07 — Seasons, days, months
**Rule:** Seasons = lower case. Days of the week, months and feast days = upper case.
✅ spring, summer, autumn, winter / Tuesday, January, Ascension Day
❌ Spring, Summer / tuesday, january

### §3-08 — Permanent vs ad hoc bodies
**Rule:** Permanent bodies = upper case. Ad hoc groups = lower case.
✅ The Commission Delegation in the United States [permanent]
✅ The Polish delegation to a meeting [ad hoc]

## Chapter 4 — Geographical names

### §4-01 — UK, not Great Britain
**Rule:** Use `United Kingdom` or `UK` in geopolitical contexts. Do not use `Great Britain` or `England` to refer to the whole country.
✅ the United Kingdom / the UK
❌ Great Britain / England (when referring to the whole country)

### §4-02 — Canton: lower vs upper case
**Rule:** `the canton of` (lower case) = geopolitical area. `the Canton` (upper case) = the government of that canton.
✅ I live in the canton of Neuchâtel.
✅ The Canton of Basel approved the new legislation.

### §4-03 — Town names: use local language
**Rule:** Use the name in the language spoken in that canton. For bilingual towns, follow the source text language. Exception: always use both names for Biel/Bienne.
✅ The Federal Council travelled to Sierre and Sion. (not Siders and Sitten)

### §4-04 — Rivers
**Rule:** Prefix Swiss rivers with `River`. Preserve the local language name. Major European rivers may omit `River`.
✅ the River Aare / the River Ticino / the Rhine / the Rhone
❌ the Aare / the River Rhine

### §4-05 — Lakes
**Rule:** Use established English names where they exist.
✅ Lake Geneva / Lake Constance / Lake Lucerne
❌ Lac Léman / Bodensee / Vierwaldstättersee

### §4-06 — Compass points
**Rule:** Compass points are lower case unless they form part of a proper name.
✅ north-western Switzerland / the north of the country
✅ North Africa / the Middle East
❌ North-Western Switzerland / the North of the country

## Chapter 5 — Compound words

### §5-01 — Hyphens to disambiguate
**Rule:** Use hyphens sparingly but purposefully to clarify meaning.
✅ re-cover (cover again) vs recover / re-creation vs recreation / fifty-odd people (about fifty)
❌ fifty odd people (fifty strange people)

### §5-02 — Adverb + adjective
**Rule:** No hyphen when the adverb ends in `-ly`. Hyphen required with other adverbs.
✅ a beautifully phrased sentence / a well-known problem / an above-mentioned report
❌ a beautifully-phrased sentence / a well known problem

### §5-03 — Noun-participle modifier
**Rule:** A noun-participle modifier should be hyphenated.
✅ drug-related crime / crime-fighting unit / oil-bearing rock

### §5-04 — Noun-noun modifier
**Rule:** A noun-noun modifier does not take a hyphen.
✅ labour market liberalisation / money laundering scheme

### §5-05 — Adjective-noun modifier
**Rule:** An adjective-noun modifier should be hyphenated.
✅ first-class lounge

### §5-06 — Compound modifier before vs after noun
**Rule:** Compound phrases are hyphenated only when used adjectivally before the noun.
✅ long-term effects / policy for the long term
✅ large-scale redundancies / redundancies on a large scale
✅ up-to-date information / the information is up to date

### §5-07 — Nouns from phrasal verbs
**Rule:** Often written as one word or hyphenated. The situation is fluid.
✅ handout, takeover, comeback (solid)
✅ follow-up, run-up, spin-off, start-up (hyphenated)

### §5-08 — Present participles of phrasal verbs as attributes
**Rule:** When used as attributes, generally hyphenated.
✅ cooling-off period

### §5-09 — Prefixes before proper names
**Rule:** Prefixes before proper names are always hyphenated.
✅ pro-American, intra-EU, pan-European, trans-European
> Exception: `transatlantic` (no hyphen)

### §5-10 — Avoiding double vowels/consonants
**Rule:** Use a hyphen to avoid juxtaposing two vowels or two consonants.
✅ re-election, re-entry, re-examine, anti-intellectual, part-time

### §5-11 — Spelled-out numbers and fractions
**Rule:** Spelled-out numbers take hyphens. Fractions take a hyphen when used attributively, but not as nouns.
✅ twenty-eight / two-thirds completed / an increase of two thirds
❌ twentyeight / two thirds completed / a twothirds increase

### §5-12 — Coordination of hyphenated compounds
**Rule:** Hyphenated compounds may be coordinated with a suspended hyphen. Non-hyphenated (closed) compounds must be written out in full.
✅ Technology- and science-based start-ups
✅ macrostructural and microstructural changes (not macro- and microstructural)

### §5-13 — One word or two: standard list
**Rule:** Use the following spellings for consistency.

| One word | Two words |
|---|---|
| counterterrorism | cyber risk |
| crowdfunding | cyber resilience |
| cybercrime | master plan |
| cybersecurity | per cent |
| cyberspace | think tank |
| database | under way |
| email | |
| factsheet | |
| healthcare | |
| peacebuilding | |
| peacekeeping | |
| roadmap | |
| smartcard | |
| wastewater | |

> ⚠️ cyber-compounds: one word unless second letter is `r` (→ `cyber risk`, `cyber resilience`)
> ⚠️ Exception: National Cyber Security Centre

## Chapter 6 — Punctuation

### §6-00 — General
**Rule:** Punctuation marks (except dashes) are closed up to the preceding word. Stops (. ? ! : ;) are followed by a single space only.
✅ He asked his assistant: 'Did you book my flight?'
❌ He asked his assistant , ' Did you book my flight ? '

### §6-01 — Colons
**Rule:** A colon introduces an expansion, list or explanation.
- Full clause before colon → second part starts lower case.
- Non-sentence before colon → second part is a complete clause starting with a capital.
- Introducing a quotation → capital after the colon.
✅ We made a few minor changes: different dates, a new venue and three new speakers.
✅ Please note: The rules on colons should be applied correctly at all times.
✅ He declared: 'The rules on colons are so complicated!'

### §6-02 — Semicolons
**Rule:** Use a semicolon to combine two closely related sentences without a conjunction, or to separate series items that themselves contain commas.
✅ The committee agreed on a final text; however, the issue of semicolons was not considered.
✅ The success hinged on the prompt and correct implementation of the initial project; the careful monitoring, updating and assessment of the agreed measures; and the ongoing cooperation among partner countries and with priority countries.

### §6-03 — Commas
**Rule:** Use a comma to separate items in a series. A serial (Oxford) comma before the final 'and'/'or' is optional but may be used for clarity. Place a comma before `etc.` when it follows a series. Non-defining relative clauses (non-essential info) take commas and use `which`; defining clauses use `that` (no commas).
✅ sugar, beef and milk products
✅ sugar, beef and veal, and milk products (Oxford comma for clarity)
✅ Sugar, beef, milk products, etc.
✅ The translations, which have been revised, can now be sent out. (non-defining)
✅ The translations that have been revised can now be sent out. (defining)

### §6-04 — Dashes
**Rule:** Use en dashes (–) for coordinate/contrasting pairs, ranges and parenthesis. Em dashes (—) are American usage — avoid in British English.
✅ London – as you know – is a melting pot of cultures.
✅ Switzerland is world-famous for watches – and chocolate.
❌ London — as you know — is a melting pot of cultures.

### §6-05 — Brackets
**Rule:** Square brackets mark insertions in quoted material. Footnote numbers appear outside punctuation, unless they refer to text within parentheses. When translating an institution name, place the translation in round brackets after the original.
✅ 'Let them [the poor] eat cake.'
✅ The consumption of raw materials (the majority of which are mined in Africa¹) is high.²
✅ The Ecole polytechnique fédérale Lausanne (Federal Institute of Technology in Lausanne) is hosting a conference.

### §6-06 — Inverted commas
**Rule:** Use `'...'` (single) or `"..."` (double). Never use `«...»`, `„..."` or `'...'`. Use double inverted commas for direct speech; single for speech within speech or to isolate a word/phrase. In reported speech, punctuation goes inside the inverted commas. Do not use inverted commas around organisation or programme names (unlike French/German).
✅ 'Brexit means Brexit,' May announced.
✅ Thatcher once declared: 'There is no such thing as society!'
✅ The next EU Framework Programme for Research and Innovation is called Horizon Europe.
❌ «Brexit means Brexit» / The next EU Framework Programme is called 'Horizon Europe'.

### §6-07 — Apostrophes
**Rule:** Plurals of abbreviations and figures/decades do not take an apostrophe.
✅ SMEs, UFOs, the 1980s, 747s
❌ SME's, UFO's, the 1980's, 747's
> Exception: plurals of lower-case single letters may take an apostrophe to avoid misreading: `dot your i's`.

## Chapter 7 — Numbers

### §7-01 — Words or figures
**Rule:** Numbers up to ten are written out in continuous text. Numbers above ten may be written in figures. Never begin a sentence with a figure. If a passage contains both, use either figures or words for all numbers.
✅ The committee discussed ten items. / They discussed 27 items.
✅ Twenty-seven items were discussed. (not: 27 items were discussed at the start of a sentence)

### §7-02 — Units of measurement
**Rule:** Always use figures with units of measurement denoted by symbols or abbreviations. If the unit is spelled out, figures are still acceptable.
✅ 250kW / 5 °C / EUR 50
✅ 250 kilowatts / five degrees Celsius / fifty euros

### §7-03 — Hundreds, thousands, millions
**Rule:** Hundred and thousand: figures or words. Million and billion may be combined with figures. Use a hard space between number and unit.
✅ 300 or three hundred / EUR 3,000 or three thousand euros
✅ 2.5 million / 31 billion
❌ 3 hundred / EUR 3 thousand

### §7-04 — Figures combined with words
**Rule:** Avoid combining single-digit figures with words using hyphens. Exception: set phrases.
✅ a three-year period / a five-door car
❌ a 2-hour journey
> Exception: 40-hour week, 24-hour clock

### §7-05 — Abbreviating million and billion
**Rule:** Use `m` and `bn` (no space) for sums of money in tables. Write out in full in running text.
✅ 300m / 5bn (tables)
✅ 21.2 million people (running text)

### §7-06 — Decimal point
**Rule:** English uses a decimal point, not a comma.
✅ 4.5
❌ 4,5

### §7-07 — Thousands separator
**Rule:** In running text, use a comma. In tables and diagrams, use a hard space.
✅ 3,000 (running text) / 30 000 (tables)
❌ 3'000 / 3.000

### §7-08 — Spacing between number and unit
**Rule:** No space between number and unit, except for degrees Celsius and Fahrenheit (hard space).
✅ 50kg / 100% / 60m / 15 °C / 120 °F
❌ 50 kg / 15°C

### §7-09 — Chemical notation
**Rule:** Subscripted numerals in chemical symbols should be subscripted.
✅ CO₂ / C₃N₂ / H₂O
❌ CO2 / C3N2 / H2O

### §7-10 — Coordinates
**Rule:** Symbols of measurement are set close up to the figure. Hard space between figure and compass point.
✅ 50° N / 15° 7' 5" W

### §7-11 — Ordinals
**Rule:** Write ordinal suffixes on the line, not superscript.
✅ 1st, 2nd, 4th
❌ 1ˢᵗ, 2ⁿᵈ, 4ᵗʰ

## 8. Currency

### §8-01 — ISO currency codes
**Rule:** Use ISO 4217 currency codes, placed before the number. CHF for Swiss francs, EUR for euros. Alternatively, write out in full.
✅ CHF 50 / fifty Swiss francs / EUR 200 / two hundred euros
❌ SFr. 50 / Fr. 50

### §8-02 — Centimes, not Rappen or cents
**Rule:** The franc is divided into 100 centimes. Do not use "Rappen" or "cents".
✅ CHF 20.50 (twenty francs and fifty centimes)
❌ CHF 20.50 (twenty francs and fifty Rappen)

## 9. Fractions

### §9-01 — Write fractions out in words
**Rule:** Write fractions out in full. Do not use figures.
✅ two thirds
❌ 2/3

### §9-02 — Hyphen in adjectival/adverbial fractions
**Rule:** Hyphenate fractions used as adjectives or adverbs. No hyphen when used as a noun.
✅ a two-thirds increase / two-thirds completed
✅ an increase of two thirds
❌ a two thirds increase

## 10. Percentages

### §10-01 — "per cent" in running text
**Rule:** Write "per cent" as two words in running text and when the number is spelled out. Use % with no space when used with figures.
✅ twenty per cent / 25%
❌ twenty % / 25 %

### §10-02 — Per cent vs percentage point
**Rule:** Observe the distinction between "per cent" (or %) and "percentage point(s)".
✅ Rates rose by two percentage points, from 40% to 42% (an increase of 5%).
❌ Rates rose by two per cent, from 40% to 42%.

## 11. Ranges

### §11-01 — Written-out ranges: repeat symbols and multiples
**Rule:** When a range is written out in full, repeat symbols and multiples on both sides.
✅ from CHF 20 million to CHF 30 million / between 10 °C and 70 °C
❌ from CHF 20 to 30 million

### §11-02 — En-dash ranges: close up, no repetition
**Rule:** When a range is indicated by an en dash, close up the dash. Do not repeat the symbol or multiple if unchanged.
✅ CHF 20–30 million / 10–70 °C / 9–11am
❌ CHF 20 – 30 million

### §11-03 — En-dash ranges: space when symbol or multiple changes
**Rule:** If the symbol or multiple changes across the range, leave a space on either side of the en dash.
✅ 100kW – 40MW / 9am – 2pm
❌ 100kW–40MW

## 12. Dates and time

### §12-01 — Date format
**Rule:** Write the day as a simple figure (no ordinal suffix), followed by the month written out, then the year. Use a hard space between elements.
✅ 25 July 2007
❌ 25th July 2007 / July 25, 2007

### §12-02 — Avoid redundancy in dates
**Rule:** Do not repeat the year if it is clear from context.
✅ On 19 January 2011 the government decided … however, on 7 February it reversed the decision.

### §12-03 — AD / BC / BCE
**Rule:** AD comes before the number; BC and BCE follow, with a space in each case.
✅ AD 2000 / 347 BC / 55 BCE

### §12-04 — Time spans with en dash
**Rule:** Use a closed-up en dash for time spans. Do not repeat the century if unchanged, but always include the decade. Financial years that do not coincide with calendar years use a forward slash.
✅ 1939–45 / 1990–96 / 1996–2006 / 2010–12 / 1990/91

### §12-05 — Prepositions with year ranges
**Rule:** Always use paired prepositions or conjunctions. Do not mix a preposition with an en dash.
✅ from 1990 to 1995 / between 1990 and 1995
❌ from 1990–95 / between 1990–95

### §12-06 — Decades
**Rule:** Write decades with a figure followed by "s", no apostrophe.
✅ the 1990s
❌ the 1990's

### §12-07 — Time of day
**Rule:** Use the 12-hour system by default (24-hour in timetables). Separate hours from minutes with a point in the 12-hour system and a colon in the 24-hour system.
✅ 1am / 4.30pm / 1:00 / 16:30
❌ 4:30pm / 16.30

### §12-08 — Abbreviation of "hour(s)"
**Rule:** Abbreviate "hour(s)" to hr(s).
✅ 2½ hrs
❌ 2½ hours

### §12-09 — Year in titles
**Rule:** In the titles of reports and programmes, place the year in initial position.
✅ The 2017 Annual Report
❌ Annual Report 2017

## 13. Abbreviations

### §13-01 — First occurrence: write out in full
**Rule:** On first use, write the full term followed by the abbreviation in brackets. Thereafter, the abbreviation alone may be used.
✅ The Emissions Trading Scheme (ETS) … The ETS will involve …

### §13-02 — No improvised abbreviations; use TERMDAT
**Rule:** Do not render a foreign-language abbreviation with an improvised English one. Spell out the full English form instead. Use TERMDAT for established abbreviations of Swiss institutions.
✅ the Swiss Conference of Cantonal Ministers of Education (EDK)
❌ CCME

### §13-03 — Acronyms: no points, no article
**Rule:** Acronyms (pronounced as words) never take points and do not require an article.
✅ SECO, FINMA, ETS
❌ S.E.C.O. / the SECO

### §13-04 — Initialisms: no points, preceded by "the"
**Rule:** Initialisms (each letter pronounced separately) take no points and are preceded by "the". Apply this rule consistently for the Federal Administration.
✅ the FDFA, the BBC, the WTO
❌ the F.D.F.A.

### §13-05 — Writing acronyms: case rules
**Rule:** Acronyms of five letters or fewer are written in upper case. Acronyms of six letters or more take an initial capital followed by lower case. Exception: organisations that use upper case themselves (UNESCO), acronyms conventionally in upper case (WYSIWYG), and acronyms that have become common nouns (lower case).
✅ AIDS / Benelux / laser, radar
❌ benelux / LASER

### §13-06 — Contractions: no point
**Rule:** Contractions (middle of word omitted) do not take a point in British usage.
✅ Mr, Dr, No, Nos
❌ Mr., Dr., No.

### §13-07 — Truncations: end in a point
**Rule:** Truncations (end of word omitted) end in a point. Plural forms of truncations are contractions and take no point. Truncated forms used as codes or symbols take no point.
✅ Feb., fig. / figs (plural) / EN, kg
❌ Feb / figs.

### §13-08 — "versus": v (no point)
**Rule:** Abbreviate "versus" as v without a point.
✅ Roe v Wade
❌ Roe vs. Wade

### §13-09 — Personal name initials
**Rule:** Abbreviate first names to a single letter followed by a point (omit "h", unlike German). Separate multiple initials with a space. Use a hyphen between initials of compound first names.
✅ T. (Thomas) / G. W. Bush / A.-M. (Anne-Marie)
❌ Th. (Thomas) / G.W. Bush

### §13-10 — Foreign-language abbreviations
**Rule:** Untranslated foreign-language abbreviations retain the capitalisation conventions of the original language.
✅ GmbH / SA
❌ GMBH / sa

### §13-11 — e.g. and i.e.
**Rule:** Each letter is followed by a point. Use a comma, colon, or dash before "e.g." and "i.e.", but no comma after them. They remain lower case even at the start of a footnote. If a list begins with "e.g.", do not end it with "etc."
✅ He talked about many things, e.g. the difficulties of life in Switzerland.
❌ He talked about many things, e.g., the difficulties of life in Switzerland.

## 14. Lists

### §14-01 — Grammatical consistency
**Rule:** Each list item must be a grammatically correct continuation of the introductory sentence. Do not change syntax midstream (e.g. switching from nouns to verbs).
✅ The health centres are in charge of: providing … / taking … / cooperating …
❌ The health centres are in charge of: primary healthcare / to fight malaria / cooperation

### §14-02 — Introductory sentence: colon
**Rule:** The introductory sentence or fragment is followed by a colon.
✅ The health centres are in charge of:
❌ The health centres are in charge of

### §14-03 — Simple items: no end punctuation
**Rule:** For a list of simple items, no end punctuation is needed on each item.
✅ - apples / - pears / - bread

### §14-04 — Items completing a sentence fragment
**Rule:** When items complete the introductory fragment, begin each with a lower-case letter, use a semi-colon after each item, a full stop after the final item, and "and" or "or" before the last item.
✅ The health centres are in charge of:
- providing primary healthcare for the local population;
- taking measures to fight malaria; and
- cooperating with the local authorities.

### §14-05 — Items as complete sentences
**Rule:** When items are complete sentences, either (a) begin each with a capital letter and end with a full stop, or (b) begin each with a lower-case letter, use a semi-colon after each item, and a full stop after the last. Be consistent within a document.
✅ (a) Humanitarian needs are great. / Local capacities are insufficient.
✅ (b) humanitarian needs are great; / local capacities are insufficient.

## 15. Foreign words and phrases

### §15-01 — Italics for foreign words
**Rule:** Foreign words and phrases used in an English text are italicised (no inverted commas) and retain their original accents.
✅ *inter alia* / *raison d'être*
❌ "inter alia" / raison d'être (no italics)

### §15-02 — Common foreign words: no italics
**Rule:** Words and phrases now fully integrated into English are not italicised.
✅ role, ad hoc, per capita, per se

### §15-03 — Personal names: retain accents
**Rule:** Personal names retain their original accents.
✅ Dürrenmatt / Saint-Saëns
❌ Durrenmatt / Saint-Saens

### §15-04 — Foreign-language quotations
**Rule:** Verbatim quotations in a foreign language are placed in quotation marks and are not italicised.
✅ Mr Kohl announced: 'Ich bin ein Pekinese'.
❌ Mr Kohl announced: *Ich bin ein Pekinese*.

### §15-05 — Chinese: use pinyin
**Rule:** Use the pinyin romanisation system for Chinese place names and proper nouns.
✅ Beijing / Guangzhou / Nanjing / Xinjiang
❌ Peking / Canton / Nanking / Sinkiang

### §15-06 — False Anglicisms (German)
**Rule:** Beware of false Anglicisms that appear in German source texts. Replace them with correct English equivalents.
✅ projector / mobile phone / bullying / website / spa / happy ending
❌ Beamer / Handy / Mobbing / Homepage / Wellness / Happy End

### §15-07 — Helveticisms
**Rule:** When translating key Swiss-German terms, use the following established English equivalents (provide a gloss where needed):
- *Willensnation* → nation based on political will; nation forged by the will of the people
- *Konkordat* → formal agreement between a nation's federal government and its subdivisions
- *Konkordanz* → concordance; consensus-based decision-making process
- *Milizsystem* → part-time public service; semi-professional (in military context: non-standing units)
- *Wasserschloss* → Europe's water reservoir (never "moated castle")
- *Höchste/r Schweizer/in* → the president of the National Council
- *Internationale Genf / International Geneva* → the international institutions in Geneva (e.g. UN, ILO)

## 16. Singular or plural

### §16-01 — Collective nouns
**Rule:** Use a singular verb when the emphasis is on the entity as a whole; use a plural verb when the emphasis is on the individuals within it.
✅ The government is considering the matter.
✅ The police have failed to trace the goods.

### §16-02 — Plural-name countries and organisations: singular verb
**Rule:** Countries and organisations with a plural name take a singular verb.
✅ The United States is reconsidering its position.
✅ The United Nations was unable to reach agreement.

### §16-03 — Groups of islands: plural verb
**Rule:** Groups of islands take a plural verb.
✅ The Bahamas lie off the coast of Florida.

### §16-04 — Multiple subject forming a whole: singular verb
**Rule:** When a multiple subject clearly forms a whole, use a singular verb.
✅ Checking and stamping the forms is the job of the customs authorities.

### §16-05 — Words in -ics
**Rule:** Words in -ics are singular when denoting a scientific discipline or body of knowledge; plural in all other contexts.
✅ Economics is commonly regarded as a soft science.
✅ The economics of the new process were studied in depth.

### §16-07 — "Data"
**Rule:** "Data" takes a singular verb in general use (uncountable noun). In scientific, statistical, and financial contexts, it takes a plural verb.
✅ Little data is available to support the conclusions.
✅ The data come a day after the vote on monetary policy.

### §16-08 — "None"
**Rule:** "None" takes a singular verb with uncountable nouns or when meaning "not one / not any". It takes a plural verb when qualified (e.g. "almost none", "none but a few").
✅ None of the water is polluted. / None of us is perfect.
✅ Almost none of the members are going.

### §16-09 — Decimal fractions, weights, and money
**Rule:** Decimal fractions referring to countable items take a plural verb. Weights and sums of money take a singular verb.
✅ An average of 1.24 babies are born per woman.
✅ CHF 50 million is a lot of money.

## 17. Gender-neutral language

### §17-01 — Avoid he/she
**Rule:** Do not use he/she, his/her, and associated constructions.
❌ If a student fails his/her exam, he/she must change his/her major.

### §17-02 — Use singular "they"
**Rule:** Use singular "they" and associated pronouns to include all gender identities.
✅ One student failed their exam. They then decided to change their major.

### §17-03 — Prefer plural subject where possible
**Rule:** Where possible, make the subject plural to avoid gendered pronouns, even if the source text uses a singular subject.
✅ Employees may lose their right to social security if the contract is terminated.
❌ The employee may lose his right to social security if the contract is terminated.

### §17-04 — Gender-neutral noun forms
**Rule:** De-emphasise gender in noun forms.
✅ chair / chairperson / flight attendants / cabin crew / firefighters / police officer / tradesperson
❌ chairman / air hostesses / firemen / policeman / tradesman

### §17-05 — Second person or imperative for instructions
**Rule:** In manuals and sets of instructions, address the reader directly using the second person or the imperative form.
✅ You should first turn on your computer. / First turn on your computer.
❌ The user should first turn on his/her computer.

## 18. Correspondence

### §18-01 — Salutation/sign-off pairs
**Rule:** Match the salutation to the sign-off. Unknown addressee: "Dear Sir or Madam" or "To whom it may concern" → "Yours faithfully". Known addressee: "Dear Mr/Ms/Mx/Dr Smith" → "Yours sincerely".
✅ Dear Ms Smith … Yours sincerely
❌ Dear Sir … Yours sincerely

### §18-02 — No punctuation after salutation or sign-off
**Rule:** In UK English, no comma or colon after the salutation or sign-off. No full stop after Mr, Ms, Mx, Dr.
✅ Dear Ms Smith / Yours sincerely
❌ Dear Ms. Smith, / Yours sincerely,

### §18-03 — Avoid "Sincerely yours"
**Rule:** "Sincerely yours" is American English. Use "Yours sincerely" instead.
✅ Yours sincerely
❌ Sincerely yours

### §18-04 — Avoid gendered salutations
**Rule:** Do not use "Ladies and gentlemen". If the recipient's title is unknown, use their first name and surname.
✅ Dear Jamie Smith
❌ Ladies and gentlemen

### §18-05 — Multiple addressees: single salutation
**Rule:** Do not replicate the German practice of separate salutations on separate lines. Use a single salutation covering all addressees.
✅ Dear Mr Khan and Ms Berger / Dear Sadiq and Anne
❌ Dear Mr Khan (line 1) / Dear Ms Berger (line 2)

## 19. Names and titles

### §19-01 — Default title: Ms
**Rule:** Use "Ms" by default unless the person concerned prefers otherwise (e.g. Mrs for a married woman who has taken her husband's surname). Note that Mme and Frau are not necessarily equivalent to Mrs.
✅ Ms Smith
❌ Mrs Smith (unless the person specifically uses Mrs)

### §19-02 — Names in running text
**Rule:** Spell out the first name in full on first mention. Use title + surname thereafter. Do not repeat the first name or lengthy titles in running text.
✅ Simonetta Sommaruga (first mention) → Ms Sommaruga (thereafter)
✅ Ueli Maurer, President of the Swiss Confederation (first mention) → Mr Maurer (thereafter)

### §19-03 — Foreign-language titles: simplify
**Rule:** Avoid titles not customary in English. Drop combined academic titles; retain only what is standard in English. If using Mr or Ms, be sure of the person's gender.
✅ Prof. H. Schmidt / Mr W. Braun / Ms B. De Bruyn / Mr Reuter
❌ Prof. Dr. H. Schmidt / Dipl. Ing. W. Braun / Frau B. De Bruyn / M. Reuter

### §19-04 — Dr: retain when in original, no point
**Rule:** Retain the title "Dr" when it appears in the original text (except in combined titles). As a contraction, it takes no final point.
✅ Dr K. Jones
❌ Dr. K. Jones

### §19-05 — "Wissenschaftlicher Mitarbeiter": no standard equivalent
**Rule:** There is no standard English equivalent for "Wissenschaftlicher Mitarbeiter / collaborateur scientifique". Check with the client for the person's actual role. Possible renderings: "legal consultant", "data research specialist", "desk officer", or "officer".

## 20. Names of bodies and titles of works

### §20-01 — Official names: use the established English name
**Rule:** If a body has an official name in English, always use it.
✅ World Organisation for Animal Health
❌ Organisation Mondiale de la Santé Animale

### §20-02 — Proper names: keep original, add gloss at first mention
**Rule:** If the name is essentially a proper name (e.g. a company or local association), keep it in the original language. At first mention, add an ad hoc translation or explanation in brackets.
✅ The Freiburger Wanderverein (a local Swiss hiking association) offers activities year round.

### §20-03 — Familiar foreign names: use original
**Rule:** If a body's foreign-language name is familiar to the intended readership, or the body uses it in its own English texts, use it as is rather than translating.
✅ UBS has issued a strict dress code. / Médecins Sans Frontières has long been active in this region.

### §20-04 — Standard wording for press releases and publications
**Rule:** Use the following standard translations in press releases and publications:
- *Zuständiges Departement* → Department responsible
- *Verantwortliche Einheit* → Unit responsible
- *Kontakt* → Contact
- *Für Rückfragen* → Enquiries
- *Adresse für Rückfragen* → Address for enquiries
- *Impressum* → Publication details

### §20-05 — Titles of works: use existing English translation
**Rule:** If an English translation of a title already exists, use it.

### §20-06 — Titles of works: gloss in square brackets if no translation exists
**Rule:** If no English translation exists and a translation is appropriate, place it in square brackets after the original title, with only the first word capitalised.
✅ *Mixité de genre et de métiers* [Mixture of gender and trades: Consequences for identity and working relationships]

### §20-07 — Links to non-English documents: indicate language
**Rule:** When linking to a document not available in English, state the available language(s) immediately after the document name.
✅ [Name of document] (de, fr, it)

### §20-08 — Footnotes and bibliographies: do not translate foreign titles
**Rule:** Foreign-language titles in footnotes or bibliographies are not translated.

## 21. Swiss political system

### §21-01 — "the Swiss Confederation" / "the Confederation"
**Rule:** Switzerland is officially known as "the Swiss Confederation". The federal state authorities are referred to as "the Confederation". "The federal government" may also be used when specifying its role.
✅ the Confederation / the federal government
❌ Switzerland's government / the Federal State

### §21-02 — Federal Council terminology
**Rule:** Use the following standard translations:
- *Bundesrat / Conseil fédéral* → Federal Council
- *die Exekutive / le pouvoir exécutif* → the executive
- *die Schweizer Regierung / le gouvernement suisse* → the Swiss government

### §21-03 — President of the Swiss Confederation: title not used with a name
**Rule:** "President of the Swiss Confederation" is not used in conjunction with a name. On first mention, give the full title followed by the name. Thereafter, use Mr/Ms + surname.
✅ the President of the Swiss Confederation, Mr Ueli Maurer, today met his Turkish counterpart … Mr Maurer
❌ President Maurer / Bundespräsident Maurer

### §21-04 — Swiss government titles
**Rule:** Use the following standard translations:
- *Bundesrat / Conseiller fédéral* → Federal Councillor
- *Bundeskanzlerin / Chancelière de la Confédération* → Federal Chancellor
- *Vizekanzler / Vice-chancelier* → Vice Chancellor
- *Ständerat / Conseiller aux États* → Member of the Council of States / Council of States member
- *Nationalrat / Conseiller national* → Member of the National Council / National Council member
❌ National Councillor

### §21-05 — Parliament
**Rule:** Switzerland has a bicameral parliament: the National Council and the Council of States, which together constitute the Federal Assembly. The two chambers have equal status; there is no "upper" or "lower" house. In joint session, it sits as the United Federal Assembly.
✅ the Federal Assembly / the Swiss parliament
❌ the Swiss Federal Assembly

### §21-06 — Federal courts
**Rule:** Use the following standard translations:
- *Bundesgericht / Tribunal fédéral* → Federal Supreme Court
- *Bundesstrafgericht / Tribunal pénal fédéral* → Federal Criminal Court
- *Bundesverwaltungsgericht / Tribunal administratif fédéral* → Federal Administrative Court
- *Bundespatentgericht / Tribunal fédéral des brevets* → Federal Patent Court

### §18-06 — Email formality
**Rule:** In emails, match the salutation and sign-off to the level of formality. Use "Dear" in formal contexts, "Hi" in informal ones (e.g. emailing a colleague).
✅ Dear Ms Smith … Yours sincerely
✅ Hi Jane … Best wishes / Kind regards

### §18-07 — Layout: title not on a separate line
**Rule:** In English, the recipient's title is not placed on a separate line (unlike German CD Bund letter templates).
✅ Mr Sadiq Khan / The Queens Walk / London SE1 2AA
❌ Mr Sadiq Khan (separate line) / The Queens Walk …
